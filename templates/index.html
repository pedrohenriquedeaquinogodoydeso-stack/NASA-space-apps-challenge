The problem we are solving

Design and build an interactive game or app that lets students run a lunar or Martian outpost, balancing competing demands like life support, radiation shielding, power, and food production, so they experience firsthand the decisions that determine whether a mission fails or succeeds. NASA Space Apps Challenge
  • Too simpleSpace content often hides the engineering trade-offs, so a mission looks like a matter of pressing “go”.
  • Too complexReal mission tools show the trade-offs, but at a level that loses a young learner in the first minute.
  • Not tangibleFew tools let students feel a trade-off: give up something now, and watch what it costs ninety sols later.

What we built: a Mars mission in three steps

  1. Plan it

    Pick a landing site, a rocket, a crew size and a mission length. Choose which modules fit in the payload. A live telemetry panel shows mass, power and total consumables as you change each setting.

  2. Launch it

    Your setup decides what the crew starts with. A four-person, 500-sol mission needs about 1,670 kg of oxygen, 5,000 L of water and 3,600 kg of food. The lander carries 500 kg, 1,500 L and 800 kg. The gap has to be closed by recycling, growing and making resources on site.

  3. Survive it

    Each sol brings three yes/no decisions: morning, afternoon and night. Then the day’s consumption is deducted. Stress makes science slower and incidents more likely. Your score is science points, collected while the crew stays alive.

Valles Marineris pays the most science and shields the crew best, but its canyon walls cut solar power and radio contact. Arcadia has water ice but the least science. Then there is the payload: all four optional modules weigh 5,500 kg, but only 2,800 kg of modules fit.

Every demand in the challenge is a lever in the game

Life support
Oxygen, water and food drop every sol. The water recycler recovers up to 93% of wastewater, but only while it has enough power.
Power
Three Kilopower units cover the base habitat. Every module you add draws more, solar output fades as dust builds up, and a deficit wears down the habitat.
Radiation shielding
Your landing site sets the daily dose, and some sites shield you naturally. Solar-flare alerts can still spike it, so a wrong call costs structure and nerves.
Food production
The greenhouse grows food and adds oxygen, but it is the heaviest module and needs 15 kWe for lights and heat. The MOXIE unit makes oxygen from the Martian air for 20 kWe.
Fail or succeed
The mission fails if oxygen, water or food reach zero, if the habitat breaks, or if the crew’s stress hits its limit. It succeeds only if everyone survives all the sols you planned.

Real numbers, real missions

NASA reference values

Each astronaut needs 0.835 kg of oxygen, 2.5 L of water and 1.8 kg of food per day, following NASA-STD-3001. Power, mass and rocket figures come from Mars Design Reference Architecture 5.0 and the Kilopower project.

Real events

Many situations retell what really happened on Mars, such as the 2018 dust storm that silenced the Opportunity rover and the solar-conjunction blackout when Earth cannot hear Mars.

Sources you can check

Each situation is tagged as a real event, real data or an analog built on real numbers. The ones with a real basis show a badge and a source link while you play.
